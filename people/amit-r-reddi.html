Metalloproteins & Metal Homeostasis

Amit R. Reddi

Made mobile heme pools visible inside cells

Professor of Chemistry & Biochemistry · Georgia Institute of Technology · Atlanta, USA

The contribution

What this work established

Reddi and colleagues developed fluorescent sensors that distinguish exchangeable heme from the larger total heme pool. In yeast, these tools revealed different heme availability across cellular compartments and linked a familiar metabolic protein, GAPDH, to heme buffering. Heme could be studied as a dynamic resource as well as an enzyme cofactor.

  • Hanna and colleagues, Fluorescent sensors reveal heme dynamics (2016) ↗
  • Georgia Tech research profile ↗

Why this matters to microbial metallomics

This gives microbial metallomics a way to ask what a cell can actually use. A total iron or heme measurement cannot establish availability at a particular destination. Reddi’s measurement tools help connect metal allocation to metabolism and signaling, which is necessary for separating abundance from functional sufficiency.

The next scientific question

The next question is how exchangeable heme changes across host-associated organisms and stress states, and which measurements can link those changes to specific biological outcomes.

Research background

Investigates the cellular mechanisms that activate metalloproteins and regulate their signaling functions. His laboratory focuses on heme trafficking and copper/zinc superoxide dismutase, using chemical biology to connect metal availability with metabolism and disease.
