Karen Pendergrass

Heavy metals, microbes & microbiome medicine

Karen Pendergrass is a microbial metallomics researcher, standards architect, and founder of Microbiome Medicine. Her work connects heavy metal exposure with microbes: how a metal reaches a microbial community, which organisms can use or tolerate it, and how those interactions may help explain disease-associated microbiome patterns.

She brings together research on metal-dependent microbial functions, the Heavy Metal Index’s organization of contamination evidence, and standards development at the Institute of Contaminant Standards. This profile connects those projects with her published frameworks, conference presentation, and writing on heavy metals and the microbiome.

The contribution

What this work established

Pendergrass develops research frameworks that connect heavy metal exposure, metal-dependent microbial functions, and disease-associated microbiome signatures. Her 2025 Beneficial Microbes Conference presentation examined these connections in endometriosis through metallomics and nutritional immunity. Her subsequent writing applies related questions to Parkinson’s disease, metabolic health, and early-life exposure. Alongside this hypothesis-driven work, she designed the Heavy Metal Index’s evidence architecture and contributes to contaminant standards at ICS.

Why this matters to microbial metallomics

Microbial metallomics asks what a metal does within a living system, rather than stopping at its measured concentration. Pendergrass’s contribution is to connect that question with both microbial-signature research and traceable exposure evidence. A food contaminant, a microbial enzyme, and a disease-associated community pattern can then be examined as parts of a proposed causal chain. The result is a set of specific mechanisms and research questions, with exposure standards grounded in a separately documented evidence base.

The next scientific question

The next question is which measured changes in metal form and availability explain microbial function in a particular tissue or community. Pairing exposure measurements with enzyme activity and microbiome data would help distinguish a causal metal–microbe relationship from an association, and identify which predictions from these frameworks hold up in independent studies.

Research background

Karen Pendergrass investigates upstream metal contamination in food systems and agricultural inputs, and how these exposures may influence microbial metabolism and disease. Her work connects exposure pathways with mismetallation and metal-dependent enzyme function, with particular attention to nickel, urease, [NiFe]-hydrogenases, and nickel-dependent forms of glyoxalase I. By examining microbiome and metallomic signatures together, she explores how changes in metal availability and enzyme activity may link microbial communities and heavy-metal exposure to conditions such as endometriosis and Parkinson’s disease.

She founded Microbiome Medicine and WikiBiome, is the architect of the Heavy Metal Index, and is founder and standards architect of the Institute of Contaminant Standards (ICS). Her linked institutional and author records describe these roles.

Affiliations and projects

Microbiome Medicine

Founder and researcher. Develops and publishes frameworks connecting microbiome signatures, microbial metal handling, and host biology. Her writing examines how nutritional immunity and metal-dependent enzymes can inform new research questions.

Heavy Metal Index

Architect. Organizes traceable evidence about heavy metals in foods and other exposure sources. This connects questions about microbes and metals with the materials and pathways through which exposure occurs.

Institute of Contaminant Standards (ICS)

Founder and standards architect. Leads research and methodological systems connecting contamination evidence, food metallomics, and certification standards. These responsibilities include the architecture of the HMTc framework.

WikiBiome

Founder and scientific editor. Connects published microbial-signature evidence with a structured research resource. Comparable microbiome records provide a foundation for examining microbial functions alongside metal exposure.
