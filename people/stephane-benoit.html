Host–Pathogen & Nutritional Immunity

Stéphane L. Benoit

Turning bacterial nickel dependence into an antimicrobial target.

The contribution

What this work established

Benoit and colleagues showed that dimethylglyoxime (DMG), a nickel chelator, inhibited growth of tested multidrug-resistant Salmonella Typhimurium and Klebsiella pneumoniae strains. Separate mouse and insect experiments supplied evidence of biological benefit. A Campylobacter jejuni study showed that metal interactions can change the antimicrobial response. These findings give experimental grounding to the idea that access to an enzyme cofactor can be a therapeutic research target.

  • Benoit et al. (2019) — Nickel chelation therapy as an approach to combat multi-drug resistant enteric pathogens ↗
  • Benoit & Maier — DMG and copper toxicity in Campylobacter jejuni (2022) ↗

Why this matters to microbial metallomics

His work connects the chemistry of an enzyme cofactor with the urgent search for antimicrobials. A pathogen’s metal-dependent functions can reveal targets that its conventional antibiotic-resistance profile does not describe. This is a central promise of microbial metallomics: understanding the chemistry a microbe relies on can suggest a new way to alter its behavior. Benoit and colleagues supplied experimental grounding for pursuing that direction.

Why this belongs in the antimicrobial-resistance conversation

WHO’s 2024 list prioritizes defined pathogen–resistance combinations for research and investment. Benoit’s work belongs in that conversation because it investigates a different therapeutic concept: restricting access to a cofactor that bacterial functions require. The findings provide a starting point for asking how widely that principle applies. The WHO list establishes the public-health priority; the biochemical and infection studies below establish the mechanistic rationale.

  • WHO — Bacterial Priority Pathogens List, 2024 ↗

Where nickel biology meets WHO research priorities

Several organisms represented in the WHO list have published nickel-enzyme biology relevant to this question. These selected examples connect a public-health priority with a biochemical or infection finding; they are not a complete enzyme census, and the cited studies do not all examine the specific resistant strains named by WHO.

The mechanism is the reason to investigate

If an infection-relevant function needs nickel, and an intervention restricts the nickel pool that supplies it, that function may become a therapeutic vulnerability even when conventional antibiotics fail. This is the research hypothesis. Benoit’s work matters because it connects that biochemical idea with observed antimicrobial effects.

A chelator’s affinity is part of the argument. Biological activity also depends on which nickel pool it can reach and how that pool supplies the relevant enzyme. Restricting access during enzyme assembly is a different proposition from extracting nickel already bound inside an active enzyme. Following those connections is how microbial metallomics turns a chemical property into a biological explanation.

An enzyme name starts the question

“Glyoxalase I positive” is not synonymous with nickel-dependent: distinct metal-activation classes exist. Likewise, the nickel-bearing hydrogenases are the [NiFe] class; other hydrogenase classes use iron without nickel. Enzyme presence, its actual cofactor, its activity in a host, and its contribution to infection are separate links in the argument.

A metabolic function can matter for stress survival or persistence without already having been established as a virulence factor in every organism. Identifying that missing connection is valuable foundational science. It gives a promising idea a specific next question instead of treating the absence of a completed chain as a reason to stop.

What would move the explanation forward?

The mechanistic prediction is a relationship between altered nickel availability, reduced activity of the proposed nickel-dependent function, and a meaningful biological effect. A response explained equally well by unrelated toxicity would support a different account. A relevant function sustained through another metal or pathway would help explain limited response. These alternatives make the hypothesis more informative.

The larger opportunity is to connect pathogen biology, the host’s metal regulation, and the surrounding community. A community’s metal-handling functions may reveal dependencies that a species list or an antibiotic-resistance label leaves invisible. Establishing which of those dependencies matter is a research agenda for microbiome medicine—and a reason these pioneers deserve attention.

The next scientific question

The next research question is where nickel-directed approaches offer a useful and selective effect in the biological settings that matter. Explaining which aspects of metal handling predict response—and which predict failure—would turn a promising proof of concept into a more informative therapeutic strategy. Host effects and the wider microbial community belong in that explanation from the beginning.

Research background

Investigates nickel acquisition, storage and nickel-dependent enzymes in bacterial pathogens. His work on Helicobacter and Campylobacter connects hydrogenase and urease biology with host colonization, respiratory metabolism and iron–sulfur protein maturation. He has also explored nickel chelation against multidrug-resistant enteric bacteria in laboratory and animal-model studies.
