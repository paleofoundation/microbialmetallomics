Who’s Who · Discoveries and their significance

The people changing what we can explain.

The discoveries behind microbial metallomics explain how metals power microbial life, how metal competition changes infection, and how the wrong metal can disable a biological function. Meet the people making those connections measurable—and opening new possibilities for medicine and environmental science.

Why the field matters

Microbiome medicine needs the chemistry behind the organisms.

A taxonomic profile can identify organisms without revealing whether their enzymes have the right cofactors, whether the host is withholding a nutrient, or whether a contaminant has changed chemical form. Microbial metallomics supplies that layer of explanation—and uses it to identify what to investigate next. These researchers are building the mechanisms, measurements, and therapeutic concepts through which microbiome medicine can move from describing a community to understanding its functions and changing its effects.

New antimicrobial targets

Benoit’s nickel-chelation research connects enzyme chemistry to antimicrobial effects. That proof of concept creates a reason to investigate metal-dependent functions across resistant organisms, including taxa represented in WHO’s priority list.

Why the wrong metal matters

An enzyme can be present yet unable to do its job because the metal occupying its active site is unsuitable. Mismetallation links metal competition to a specific failure of biological function—something an organism count or total-metal result can miss.

Follow the mechanism →

Seeing the relevant metal pool

Speciation and imaging researchers reveal which chemical forms are present and where they accumulate. Those measurements can distinguish competing explanations of the same exposure or microbiome pattern—and point research toward the relevant biological pool.

From everyday exposure to a biological mechanism

For foods, agricultural inputs, textiles, orthodontic appliances, and jewellery, useful investigation follows a sequence: what metal is released, in what chemical form, into which compartment, and with what effect? The researchers in this directory make different parts of that sequence accessible. The amount detected is a starting point; local chemistry and biological response give it meaning.

Mismetallation provides a concrete molecular example. Gu and Imlay connected oxidative stress in bacteria to loss of catalytic iron, occupation by zinc, and reduced enzyme activity. This mechanism suggests a sharper exposure question: could local metal competition disrupt a function even when a bulk measurement looks unremarkable? The relevant concentration, compartment, and enzyme activity are the links to investigate.

Piercing-related nickel sensitization concerns an immune response. A Danish schoolgirl study found that the association with piercing differed before and after nickel-exposure regulation. The contact route and material therefore belong in the explanation. This points toward exposure prevention without implying that every pierced child develops allergy or that the response has a demonstrated microbial cause.

Orthodontic research illustrates why the compartment matters. One study found higher nickel in dental plaque and a retained saliva fraction in patients with appliances, while filtered saliva did not show the same difference. A separate study of specific dental alloys found measurable release without patch-test reactions in the nickel-sensitive participants tested. These findings invite a local biological question: what does metal retained at an interface do to the functions of the community living there? Metal release, microbial effects, and sensitization describe distinct parts of that investigation.
