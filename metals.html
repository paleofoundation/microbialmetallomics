The metals atlas

A wider view of the microbial periodic table.

From nickel enzymes to arsenic transformation, rare-earth metabolism, and metal-based materials: 62 profiles covering 70 elements, with mechanisms, selected research, and the limits of the evidence.

Reviewed September 30, 2026 · Curated primary literature · Open reference links

One element can play more than one role.

Microbes can acquire, exclude, transform, store, or precipitate metals. A cofactor can become toxic in excess; a contaminant can support a specialized microbial process. These groups are reading pathways, not mutually exclusive chemical or safety classifications.

“Heavy metal” has no single boundary used consistently across the literature. We include relevant metalloids, light metals, and selenium and identify their context. The atlas covers selected documented interactions; it does not claim to contain every online paper or establish a disease pathway for every element.

Microbial cofactors

Enzyme chemistry, acquisition, competition, and toxicity. Requirements differ among organisms.

Vanadium

Atomic number 23

Alternative nitrogenase and microbial vanadium reduction

Vanadium has a defined cofactor role in certain nitrogen-fixing microbes. Other bacteria transform vanadium between oxidation states, making it relevant to both enzyme chemistry and environmental metal cycling.

Atomic number 25

Oxidative-stress defense and environmental manganese cycling

Manganese can support microbial resistance to oxidative stress, while its accessibility is contested by the host. Environmental microbes also transform manganese oxidation states, linking microbial energy metabolism to mineral formation.

Fe

Iron

Atomic number 26

Iron acquisition, iron-sulfur enzymes, and environmental respiration

Iron connects intracellular enzyme chemistry with competition for nutrients and environmental redox cycling. Its biological availability depends on its oxidation state, ligands, and mineral form; measuring total iron alone does not describe what a microbe can access.

Co

Cobalt

Atomic number 27

Corrinoid sharing, vitamin B12 uptake, and metal interference

Cobalt is central to cobalamin and related corrinoids, which form a shared but chemically diverse resource in microbial communities. Free cobalt exposure is a different biological question from access to intact vitamin B12 or its analogs.

Mechanisms, evidence & sources → for Cobalt
Ni

Nickel

Atomic number 28

Urease, [NiFe]-hydrogenases, and nickel-dependent glyoxalase I

Nickel supports distinct microbial enzyme systems, including urease and [NiFe]-hydrogenases. Some glyoxalase I enzymes prefer nickel, but that property is not shared by all glyoxalase I proteins; metal identity must be established for the organism and enzyme in question.

Mechanisms, evidence & sources → for Nickel
Cu

Copper

Atomic number 29

Methane oxidation, copper acquisition, and metal toxicity

Copper supports specialized microbial chemistry while excess accessible copper damages sensitive cellular targets. Its microbial roles span methane-oxidizing environmental bacteria and the metal-defense systems of organisms such as E. coli.

Mechanisms, evidence & sources → for Copper
Zn

Zinc

Atomic number 30

Nutrient limitation, metal competition, and colonization resistance

Zinc is a microbial nutrient whose excess can also impair other metal-dependent processes. Its effects depend on the balance between accessible zinc and competing nutrients, not simply on the amount of zinc present.

Mechanisms, evidence & sources → for Zinc
Mo

Molybdenum

Atomic number 42

Molybdenum-cofactor respiration and nitrogen fixation

Molybdenum enables microbial reactions through specialized cofactors, including respiratory molybdoenzymes and the distinct FeMo cofactor of nitrogenase. These pathways connect metal availability with anaerobic growth and environmental nitrogen cycling.

Mechanisms, evidence & sources → for Molybdenum
W

Tungsten

Atomic number 74

Tungstoenzymes and competition with molybdenum

Tungsten is a functional cofactor in some microbes, including gut-associated organisms. It also competes with molybdenum-dependent pathways, so its effect depends on which metal-utilizing enzymes a species possesses.

Coverage & evidence gaps

The atlas spans microbial biochemistry, environmental communities, host-associated microbiomes, and experimental materials. The depth of each profile follows the available sources: a biochemical assay, a soil experiment, an animal model, and a human trial answer different questions.

Rare lanthanides are grouped when evidence is limited or shared across a series. Elements without a separately sourced interaction here are not presumed biologically irrelevant. Newly published studies, unindexed work, contradictory findings, and additional chemical species may change these summaries.

To propose an addition, send the element, chemical form, organism or community, study design, and a DOI or other primary-source link through Contribute or correct. A paper showing metal binding alone is not proof of a nutritional requirement; a taxonomic association alone is not proof of disease causation.
