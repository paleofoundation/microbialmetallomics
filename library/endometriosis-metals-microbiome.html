Endometriosis at the metal–microbe–immune interface

Low-nickel dietary responses, red-meat and heme-iron associations, menstrual iron, and microbial inflammatory signals give endometriosis research several connected points of entry. Microbial metallomics asks how metal availability could link these findings—and which functions an intervention could change.

The working explanation

Dietary response gives the metal hypothesis a clinical foothold.

A low-nickel dietary pilot reported improvements in gastrointestinal and gynecological symptoms. A large prospective cohort linked red meat and heme iron with endometriosis diagnosis. Alongside evidence of altered local iron handling and microbial inflammatory signals, these findings motivate a connected model of exposure, host response, and microbial function. The scientific opportunity is to identify metal-responsive disease subgroups and explain why changing an input changes symptoms. This is a synthesis of existing findings into testable mechanisms. [1] [2] [3] [4]

What microbial metallomics adds

Microbiome Medicine’s approach brings disease-associated microbial signatures, functional mechanisms, and intervention outcomes into the same explanation. Concordance across those lines of evidence can identify a causal hypothesis before all intermediate steps have been measured in one study. This synthesis develops that approach by treating metal availability and host nutritional immunity as possible organizing variables. [5]

The contribution is a set of mechanistic questions: could nickel sensitivity and nickel-dependent microbial chemistry identify different responders to the same dietary change? Could heme intake and host iron retention help explain a microbial inflammatory niche? Could an intervention alter virulence or inflammatory signaling without eliminating the organism? Those questions connect nutritional epidemiology, microbial physiology, and host metal biology.

Bradford Hill’s viewpoints and modern triangulation support reasoning across evidence with different sources of bias. Clinical responses contribute to that reasoning even when their mediator is unresolved. Measuring the mediator can then distinguish explanations and improve intervention design. [8] [9]

Low nickel and lower red-meat exposure belong at the center

Low nickel: an exposure-linked clinical signal

Borghini and colleagues studied women with endometriosis and substantial gastrointestinal symptoms. After three months of a low-nickel diet, completers reported reductions in gastrointestinal symptoms, chronic pelvic pain, dysmenorrhea, and dyspareunia; the three gynecological pain outcomes each had P<.005. Of 31 completers, 28 had a positive nickel oral mucosa patch test. The dietary phase enrolled 47 women; 16 withdrew. Its uncontrolled, open-label design limits attribution, but the observed response makes nickel sensitivity and exposure serious candidates for explaining a symptomatic subgroup. [1]

The immune-susceptibility evidence also has a temporal component. Yuk and colleagues found more prior nickel-allergy diagnoses among later endometriosis cases than controls: 8/997 versus 13/3,988, adjusted OR 2.47, 95% CI 1.02–5.99. This establishes the order of recorded diagnoses, rather than disease onset. Together with the dietary response, it makes host nickel sensitivity a concrete phenotype to investigate alongside microbial nickel use. [10]

Read alongside the blood-nickel association and nickel-sensitive host and enzyme chemistry, the pilot supplies a response-to-change observation. A follow-up can ask whether responders show altered metal exposure, host inflammatory signaling, microbial activity, or a combination. A direct host effect and a microbial effect could reinforce each other; discovering their relative contribution is part of the field’s purpose. [11] [6] [7]

Red meat and heme iron: the chemical form matters

Yamamoto and colleagues followed 81,908 Nurses’ Health Study II participants and recorded 3,800 laparoscopically confirmed diagnoses. More than two daily red-meat servings versus at most one weekly serving was associated with higher risk: RR 1.56, 95% CI 1.22–1.99. Heme iron also showed a positive association: highest versus lowest quintile RR 1.30, 95% CI 1.17–1.44. Mutual adjustment attenuated both associations without eliminating them. This makes iron form relevant to the dietary question, while leaving correlated meat constituents and host pathways to distinguish. [2]

Earlier Italian case-control data also associated higher red-meat intake with endometriosis; a US population-based study did not. These comparisons keep replication and exposure range part of the research question. [12] [13]

There is also intervention evidence for a dietary pattern that excluded red meat. In Ott and colleagues’ uncontrolled study of 68 women, a Mediterranean-style regimen changed several food groups and improved reported pain over five months; 43 adhered for the full period. It supports studying diet as a modifiable input, while the combined regimen cannot isolate red-meat reduction or heme iron as the active component. [14]

The proposed metal model has two connected locations to examine: dietary heme exposure in the gut and menstrual iron processing in the pelvis. Host transport and retention connect iron compartments, so dietary intake and local microbial access need to be measured separately. This model predicts that chemical form and compartment will explain more than a total-iron value alone. It also asks whether nickel responsiveness identifies a subgroup rather than assuming every beneficial dietary pattern works through the same metal. [15] [16] [3]

Low-nickel and lower-red-meat diets test different inputs. The endometriosis low-nickel pilot did not isolate red-meat reduction, and a low-nickel H. pylori trial explicitly allowed beef. Food labels therefore cannot stand in for metal composition. A mechanistic dietary study needs to track nickel, heme iron, and the other changed nutrients separately. [1] [17]

Iron matters because the pool and the cell matter

Retrograde menstrual material offers a route by which endometrial cells, heme-containing material, and bacterial products could reach the pelvis together. Human studies found higher peritoneal-fluid iron and ferritin in defined settings and more stored iron in peritoneal macrophages. This makes local metal handling a biological starting point. It does not mean that every patient has high circulating iron or that all detected iron is available to microbes. [15] [16] [4]

Nutritional immunity changes distribution. Retaining iron inside a host cell can reduce what an extracellular organism can acquire while changing the resources encountered by an intracellular organism. The proposed microbial explanation therefore needs a location: which organism, outside or inside which cell, using heme, transferrin-bound iron, ferritin-associated iron, or a labile pool? None can be inferred from total iron alone.

Lactoferrin and ferritin also need compartment-specific interpretation. In a paired plasma/peritoneal study, the ferritin ratio distinguished cases from controls, but the lactoferrin ratio did not distinguish all cases; a stage comparison did differ. Concentration is not the same as iron saturation, bacterial access, or demonstrated antimicrobial function. [18]

The inference should explain the discordance as well as the positive findings. Geography, fertility-clinic selection, disease phenotype, cycle phase, exposure timing, and specimen type can alter the comparison. They are variables to measure, not automatic explanations that rescue a favored hypothesis.

Which microbial signal occurs in which compartment?

Khan and colleagues found more E. coli colony formation in menstrual samples and higher endotoxin in menstrual and peritoneal fluid. This supports a local bacterial-product hypothesis. Menstrual fluid is not peripheral bloodstream bacteremia; the finding does not establish bacteria surviving unrecognized in the circulation. [4]

Muraoka and colleagues supplied a stronger model-level connection: Fusobacterium enrichment in an ovarian-endometriosis-focused human cohort, TGF-β-linked conversion toward TAGLN-positive myofibroblast behavior, and antibiotic-responsive lesions in mice. This is a partial triangle connecting an organism, host mechanism, and intervention response. The metal dependence of that route was not measured. [21]

Talwar and colleagues offer a positive functional example. In 18 women with endometriosis and 31 controls, stool 4-hydroxyindole (4HI) was lower in cases, with targeted analysis confirming the finding. Administering 4HI reduced lesions, inflammation, and mechanical hypersensitivity in mice; benefits also appeared in established-lesion models and xenografts made with immortalized human endometriotic cells. This connects a human metabolite signature with an intervention and a disease phenotype. The work supports the proposed direction of research while leaving human therapeutic benefit and metal mediation open. [22]

A useful disease signature need not occur in every patient. It must identify the subgroup and location in which its proposed functions are present, active, and relevant. Absolute abundance, viability, microbial products, and host response answer different parts of that question.

Nickel can connect microbial products with host responsiveness

Nickel offers two potentially interacting routes into the model: effects on microbial functions and effects on the host response to microbial products. Schmidt and colleagues showed that nickel ions can activate human TLR4, a receptor involved in sensing LPS. Nickel recognition differed from LPS recognition, and ordinary mouse TLR4 did not respond in the same way. [6]

Oblak and colleagues found nickel–LPS synergy in cells. This adds a specific explanation for a nickel-responsive inflammatory subgroup: a given bacterial-product exposure could provoke a larger host response when nickel is present. The experiments establish receptor biology; its contribution in endometriosis is a disease-specific question. [27]

The resulting predictions differ but can coexist. A microbial route predicts altered microbial activity or endotoxin with nickel exposure. A host-response route predicts altered inflammatory responsiveness even with similar microbial abundance. Comparing both routes could help explain the reported dietary responses and identify which response markers to follow.

The glyoxalase idea has a biochemical foothold

E. coli provides a verified nickel-activated glyoxalase example. Its glyoxalase I was optimally activated by nickel in biochemical experiments. Glyoxalase helps process the glutathione adduct of methylglyoxal, a reactive carbonyl compound. This supplies a concrete candidate link between metal availability and microbial carbonyl defense. [7]

A separate study established that group B Streptococcus glyoxalase A contributes to methylglyoxal tolerance, resistance to neutrophil killing, and survival in mouse blood. Its nickel dependence remained unresolved. Putative nickel-transport annotations did not settle cofactor identity. Group B Streptococcus is Gram-positive, so it cannot also provide an LPS arm of the explanation. [28]

Together these findings justify asking whether nickel-supported carbonyl defense matters in an endometriosis-associated organism. They do not demonstrate that link in the same strain or tissue. Resistance to immune killing is also different from failure of immune recognition, and bloodstream persistence is not established here as a defining feature of endometriosis.

Low oxygen does not uniquely identify fermentation. In an inflamed-gut model, host-derived nitrate supported E. coli through anaerobic respiration. In endometriosis, enhanced glycolysis and lactate production have been measured in host peritoneal mesothelial cells. Host lactate therefore cannot be assigned to bacterial metabolism without further evidence. [29] [30]

The direction of metabolic intervention matters too. The microbial fermentation product n-butyrate reduced lesion growth in mouse models and growth of human endometriotic cells. The useful objective is to change a harmful function in a defined niche, while preserving functions that restrain inflammation or competitors. [31]

The wider dietary pattern can refine the metal explanation

The prospective Nurses’ Health Study II analysis included 70,835 women and 2,609 laparoscopically confirmed incident cases. At least one daily serving of citrus, including citrus juices, was associated with lower risk than less than one weekly serving: RR 0.78, 95% CI 0.69–0.89. Cruciferous vegetables showed a positive trend, but the extreme-category estimate was less precise: RR 1.13, 95% CI 0.95–1.34. Total vegetable intake was not associated with risk. [32]

The opposing food associations give a metal model a pattern to explain. Nickel hyperaccumulation in particular Brassicaceae, including Streptanthus polygaloides, establishes that plant chemistry can vary markedly. To connect this with ordinary cruciferous foods, the useful test is measured edible-tissue nickel and individual exposure alongside fiber, glucosinolates, preparation, and other constituents. The original diet study measured food intake rather than nickel or microbial mediation. [32] [33]

Smoking also needs a time window. An ENDO analysis using presurgical cotinine found no adult-smoking association after adjustment: RR 1.0, 95% CI 0.7–1.6. A Swedish birth cohort instead found associations with maternal smoking during pregnancy. These do not support one interchangeable smoking signal or identify nickel, cadmium, lead, or microbes as its mediator. [34] [35]

Citrus and crucifer findings came from the same underlying Nurses’ Health Study II as the red-meat analysis; they broaden the dietary comparison rather than independently replicate it. Together they motivate a chemically specified dietary hypothesis: track which metal forms and microbial substrates actually change, then ask which changes predict symptoms or disease risk. [32] [2]

An intervention can improve pain while leaving lesion burden or the proposed microbial pathway unchanged. Those are different outcomes. Likewise, a negative postoperative pain trial weighs against that regimen’s expected benefit without proving that every microbial mechanism is absent. The next clinical claim should specify the phenotype, mediator, and endpoint in advance.

Interventions can test several different microbial vulnerabilities

The examples below explain how the framework can work. They are evidence from their named systems; none supplies a complete endometriosis clinical mechanism. Killing, reducing virulence, changing resource access, and altering host signaling are different effects and should be recorded separately.

A clinical precedent for dietary metal restriction and microbial treatment

H. pylori supplies a relevant nickel-dependent microbial precedent. Campanale and colleagues’ randomized pilot added a low-nickel diet to antibiotic treatment and reported eradication in 22/26 participants versus 12/26 with antibiotics alone. A later randomized study by Brito and colleagues, which excluded nickel allergy, found higher eradication among protocol completers (91.7% versus 75.8%); its intention-to-treat comparison was not statistically significant (76.4% versus 66.2%, P=.199). These trials give dietary metal restriction a human microbial-treatment rationale. They test an adjunct to antibiotics in gastric infection; they do not identify the organism or route behind the endometriosis dietary response. [39] [17]

This precedent changes the research question from whether a dietary metal could matter at all to where, for whom, and through which process it matters. Endometriosis research can compare nickel-sensitive immune responses with metal-dependent microbial activity, using the reported symptom response to select informative hypotheses.

Fungal interactions now have a disease-specific foothold. Talwar and colleagues found altered gut fungal–bacterial co-abundance patterns in women with endometriosis; fungal depletion reduced disease progression in mice. This supports investigating community interactions. It does not yet identify Candida shielding a named pathogen in pelvic lesions, metal mediation, or a human antifungal benefit. [40]

The research program: explain response through metal-dependent functions

Human exposure + dietary response + mechanistic foundations

A nickel-responsive subgroup is a substantive research target.

Blood-nickel and prior-allergy associations, symptom changes during a low-nickel diet, and nickel-sensitive host signaling give this model several points of support. [11] [1] [6] [27] [10]

Research direction. Compare how nickel exposure, host responsiveness, and microbial function track symptom response; distinguish immune and microbial contributions.

Dietary association + local host mechanism

Iron form and routing could connect diet to disease biology.

The heme-iron association adds a dietary dimension to local iron storage and hepcidin–ferroportin biology. [2] [16] [3]

Research direction. Determine whether dietary heme, gut function, and pelvic iron handling covary in the relevant subgroup; separate intake from the iron pool available to a microbe.

Human observations + host-cell mechanism

Local iron redistribution can amplify inflammation.

Peritoneal iron storage and the hepcidin–ferroportin mechanism give this part of the model a direct biological foundation. [16] [3]

Research direction. Test whether microbial iron-acquisition activity and inflammation differ with host iron retention in the affected compartment.

Human signature + preclinical intervention

A depleted microbial metabolite can identify a functional intervention.

Lower stool 4HI in the human comparison led to supplementation that improved several disease-related outcomes in mouse models. [22]

Research direction. Follow the relevant metabolite producers and host response in independent human populations, and test clinical effects; metal mediation is an additional research question.

Partial convergence; human replication differs

A defined microbe can change endometrial cell behavior.

Fusobacterium–TGF-β work connects organism, host phenotype, and animal intervention; a later human cohort did not reproduce enrichment. [21] [23]

Research direction. Identify the disease subgroup in which organism, host-cell response, and intervention outcome align; assess whether metal availability explains that alignment.

Mechanistically plausible; cross-study bridge open

Nickel-supported carbonyl defense could affect microbial persistence.

E. coli nickel-activated glyoxalase and GBS glyoxalase-dependent survival establish different parts of a possible relationship. [7] [28]

Research direction. Determine whether the same disease-associated organism expresses a nickel-supported carbonyl-defense function in the relevant compartment.

Host–microbial interaction demonstrated in cells

Nickel sensitivity could amplify the effect of existing microbial products.

Human TLR4 activation and nickel–LPS synergy provide a route by which metal exposure and bacterial products could interact. [6] [27]

Research direction. Assess whether nickel-associated host responsiveness predicts symptoms or dietary response independently of microbial abundance.

Proposed research · not a completed result

Connect the measurements within the same disease subgroup

The next useful study would align the proposed cause, mediator, and outcome in time and place. A coherent result should make at least one competing explanation less likely.

  • Record endometriosis phenotype, cycle phase, treatment, diet and exposure timing; compare matched clinical groups rather than merging unrelated disease stages.
  • Pair clinically justified samples with controls for collection and extraction background; separate bacterial products from viable organisms and relative abundance from absolute load.
  • Measure local metal amount, binding and accessible pools together with microbial functional activity and host iron-routing proteins.
  • Distinguish direct nickel-associated receptor activation from changes in microbial endotoxin, and distinguish host lactate from microbial metabolic output.
  • For a disease-specific intervention, assess whether the proposed target changes before pain, lesion-related measures, or another prespecified endpoint. Measure plausible host-mediated alternatives too.

What would change the interpretation. A consistent local function–metal–host relationship that precedes benefit would strengthen the proposed route. Clinical improvement without the predicted intermediate would redirect the mechanistic explanation. Reproducible failure of a well-matched prediction should revise the model.

The field advances when an intervention result becomes a mechanistic clue: which input changed, which function responded, and which patients benefited? Connecting those observations can identify an actionable resource constraint, a host-sensitivity phenotype, or a microbial vulnerability. The present evidence supplies several starting points for that work.

Questions worth asking

What is this synthesis intended to contribute?

It organizes existing dietary, metal, microbial, and host-defense findings into explanations that predict different intervention responses. Its contribution is a mechanistic research agenda: define metal-responsive subgroups, identify the affected functions, and select measurements that distinguish them. It reports no new patient experiment and does not require every intermediate to be settled before a grounded hypothesis is useful.

Does endometriosis have one established microbiome signature?

Published findings vary with the compartment, population, disease phenotype, and analytical method. Local bacterial-product and organism-specific results coexist with larger null stool studies and non-replication. A useful signature must specify its setting and be tested independently.

Does elevated iron mean pathogens have more iron to use?

Only if the relevant chemical pool is accessible to the organism. Ferritin storage, heme, intracellular retention, protein binding, and extracellular labile iron are different resources. A bulk measurement does not identify which is available.

What does improvement on a low-nickel diet contribute to the hypothesis?

It adds a human response-to-intervention observation to nickel exposure and mechanistic findings. The pilot reported gastrointestinal and gynecological symptom improvement in a selected group. Explaining that response through host sensitivity, microbial function, or both is a concrete research objective; its uncontrolled design leaves causal attribution open.

Must mechanistic research wait for randomized trials?

No. A grounded mechanism and a discriminating prediction are useful before a definitive trial. The type of conclusion must match the evidence: a promising causal model, a demonstrated model mechanism, and a proven clinical effect are different claims.
