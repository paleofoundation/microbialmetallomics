Host metal defenses · Deep dive

Glutathione, metals and the mitochondrial reserve

A molecule can carry electrons, trap a reactive metabolite and coordinate a metal. Those jobs intersect, but they are not interchangeable evidence of detoxification.

The question that changes the interpretation

Follow the metal to its next destination

Glutathione connects antioxidant enzymes, chemical conjugation and metal handling. Mitochondrial import creates a reserve that whole-cell measurements can miss. Some drugs use glutathione in parent-drug metabolism; others generate reactive metabolites that it neutralizes. A shared drug–metal vulnerability is experimentally plausible, but depends on species, compartment, exposure and replenishment. [1] [2] [3] [4]

One molecule, several distinct jobs

Glutathione is a small peptide assembled from glutamate, cysteine and glycine. Its cysteine sulfur makes it chemically useful, but “antioxidant,” “conjugating substrate” and “metal ligand” describe different reactions. In peroxide removal, glutathione peroxidases draw reducing equivalents from reduced glutathione, GSH, producing oxidized glutathione, GSSG. Glutathione reductase uses NADPH to regenerate GSH. A falling reduced pool therefore needs interpretation alongside recycling, synthesis and export; it is not simply an inventory of molecules irreversibly spent. [5]

Yang and colleagues made the enzyme dependence experimentally concrete: GSH-depleting compounds and direct GPX4 inhibitors converged on ferroptotic death in susceptible cancer cells. GPX4 manipulation altered those responses. GSH thus supports an enzyme that controls lipid hydroperoxides; calling it merely a free-radical scavenger misses that catalytic system. Iron dependence in ferroptosis does not mean every injured mitochondrion has released a stockpile of toxic metals. [6]

Morgan and colleagues resolved Cu(I)–GSH coordination using controlled solution chemistry. Their work demonstrates why metal form, pH and ligand abundance matter. It does not turn GSH into a universal scavenger of every metal. Indeed, Maryon and colleagues found that lowering GSH reduced initial copper uptake through the human CTR1 system in cultured cells; restoring the pool restored uptake. A ligand can help receive and distribute an essential metal, not merely keep it out. Intracellular buffering also says nothing by itself about whether that metal leaves the body. [9] [10]

Mitochondria need delivery, not just a cellular supply

Wang, Yen, Zhu, Timson and colleagues identified SLC25A39 as necessary for mitochondrial GSH import in mammalian cells. Knockout lowered mitochondrial GSH while whole-cell GSH was preserved. Isotope-labelled GSH uptake into isolated mitochondria, genetic rescue and transport-site mutations supplied mechanistic evidence beyond correlation. SLC25A40 provided related, partly redundant capacity. The practical lesson for research is striking: a reassuring whole-cell assay can conceal a deficient organelle. [1]

Shi and colleagues independently connected SLC25A39 to the mitochondrial iron importer SLC25A37 using paired gene perturbations in K562 cells. Their interactions changed with the cells’ metabolic environment, linking GSH delivery, iron supply and oxidative phosphorylation. Liu and colleagues subsequently showed feedback through the mitochondrial protease AFG3L2 and an iron–sulfur-associated region of SLC25A39: GSH availability helps regulate transporter turnover. These discoveries make “reserve” a dynamic property of synthesis, recycling, delivery and demand, rather than a single blood or tissue concentration. [11] [12]

That architecture motivates a specific question: can a cell replenish cytosolic GSH while mitochondrial demand outruns import? This is a proposed explanation to test, not a demonstrated consequence of ordinary medicine use. It also predicts recovery and compensation; treating every conjugation event as a permanent loss ignores the adaptive machinery. [5] [1] [12]

Iron supply has potentially opposing consequences in this framework. It supports essential cofactor-dependent metabolism, yet poorly controlled availability can contribute to injury. A perturbation that improves cell survival in one growth medium may still compromise respiratory function in another. The paired-transporter studies therefore make functional readouts essential: a useful reserve model must explain energy production and recovery as well as survival, and it must specify the metabolic state in which its prediction holds. [11]

Place glutathione alongside the host’s other metal defenses →

Iron, copper and cadmium do not tell the same story

Tsvetkov and colleagues identified a copper-dependent death mechanism involving copper binding to lipoylated metabolic proteins, their aggregation and loss of iron–sulfur proteins. GSH depletion increased copper-dependent vulnerability in their experimental systems. This supplies a reason to examine copper availability alongside mitochondrial reserve. It does not establish that every low-GSH state causes this pathway, or that copper toxicity and iron-dependent lipid peroxidation are interchangeable. [13] [6]

Cadmium provides a useful time-course warning. Nair and colleagues reported GSH loss during acute Cd(II) exposure of rat proximal-tubule cells, but both GSH and GSSG rose after subchronic exposure in rats, without a changed ratio. The designs differed, so this is not a controlled acute-versus-chronic comparison. It nevertheless defeats a simple diagnostic rule that metal injury must always coincide with low measured GSH. Compensatory synthesis, survival selection and compartment-specific changes remain possibilities requiring direct measurement. [14]

Which medicines actually require glutathione for clearance?

The answer is drug-specific, and sometimes yes. Busulfan is a clear example: its prescribing information describes predominant metabolism through GSH conjugation. Gibbs and colleagues demonstrated catalysis by human liver cytosolic glutathione S-transferases. Here GSH participates in processing the parent drug. That is different from mainly removing a small reactive branch product of another drug’s metabolism. Even this positive example establishes a metabolic route, not a general rule that GSH use makes a medicine dangerous. [2] [7]

Acetaminophen, also called paracetamol, is an analgesic and antipyretic; it is not conventionally an NSAID. Its principal metabolic routes are glucuronidation and sulfation. Oxidation produces the reactive metabolite NAPQI, which GSH can trap before it attacks cellular proteins. Thus, “glutathione is needed to detoxify NAPQI” is more precise than “acetaminophen is mainly cleared by glutathione.” Parent clearance, reactive-metabolite production and protective conjugation are related but separately measurable processes. [15] [3]

Exposure scale changes the interpretation. In Heard and colleagues’ randomized trial in selected healthy adults, repeated therapeutic acetaminophen administration caused aminotransferase elevations in some participants. Most persistent elevations resolved during continued administration, without the study’s bilirubin/INR evidence of liver dysfunction. The study did not measure metal movement or mitochondrial GSH. It therefore cannot demonstrate either a metal-mediated mechanism or the absence of every possible susceptibility; it does show why an enzyme biomarker alone should not be called progressive liver failure. [16]

NSAIDs are chemically heterogeneous. Tang and colleagues identified GSH-conjugated diclofenac metabolites in rat bile and cultured human hepatocytes, supporting reactive-metabolite formation and trapping. That does not show ordinary-use depletion, nor a class-wide NSAID effect. Bort and colleagues found early ATP impairment in diclofenac-exposed hepatocytes without an early detectable fall in GSH or rise in lipid peroxidation. Together with evidence that diclofenac itself can impair mitochondrial ATP synthesis, these findings caution against assigning all injury to GSH exhaustion and metal liberation. Unmeasured compartment-specific contributions remain possible. [8] [17]

Ethanol is primarily converted to acetaldehyde by alcohol dehydrogenase and then to acetate by aldehyde dehydrogenase. GSH is not the principal reagent clearing ethanol. Chronic exposure can nevertheless alter defensive capacity. Fernández-Checa and colleagues measured impaired mitochondrial GSH uptake in chronic ethanol-fed rats. In a separate genetic mouse study, Lu and colleagues found less chronic ethanol-associated oxidant stress with CYP2E1 deletion and restoration with human CYP2E1 expression. Neither experiment measured a human drug–metal interaction, and the older transport study did not identify SLC25A39 as its molecular cause. [18] [19] [20]

One clinically relevant intersection is already explicit in busulfan prescribing information: acetaminophen-related lowering of GSH is presented as a possible reason for reduced busulfan clearance. The wording is conditional, not a measured universal interaction size. It illustrates the correct question—whether a specified exposure changes another drug’s pharmacokinetics under specified conditions—without turning a biochemical possibility into instructions to alter medication. [2]

A concrete bridge from drug injury to mobile iron

Hu and colleagues directly investigated iron redistribution during toxic acetaminophen exposure. Primary hepatocytes from fasted male C57BL/6 mice received 10 mM acetaminophen in culture. Compartment-sensitive fluorescence and interventions supported iron movement from lysosomal/endosomal stores into cytosol and mitochondria before depolarization. Lysosome-directed iron sequestration and inhibition of mitochondrial iron uptake reduced injury. The experiment supplies a causal model of iron amplification, with indirect probes and pharmacological limitations; it is not evidence that normal use releases environmental heavy metals in people. [4]

The initiating injury also matters. Xie and colleagues’ primary human hepatocyte work documented acetaminophen-associated protein adducts, JNK signalling and mitochondrial dysfunction under high experimental exposure. These findings fit a multistep injury network. An iron contribution need not replace reactive-metabolite toxicity, and an observed metal shift might amplify damage without being its original cause. Distinguishing those roles requires timed measurements and interventions that leave drug bioactivation intact. [21] [4]

The same resource can inform a pathogen

Glutathione also connects host physiology to microbial decisions. Reniere and colleagues showed that bacterial and host-derived GSH activate the virulence regulator PrfA in Listeria monocytogenes. In the 10403S strain background, genetic and biochemical experiments connected the signal to infection; an activated PrfA variant bypassed the requirement. This is a counterstrategy to remember when calling GSH a host defense: preserving host redox capacity and changing a pathogen’s behaviour are different outcomes. The finding does not justify assuming that every microbe responds similarly. [22]

A drug–metal reserve hypothesis that can fail

The proposed hypothesis is conditional: a defined metal exposure could increase injury from a defined drug when both stress a limited mitochondrial GSH supply, even before total cellular GSH falls substantially. It predicts an interaction beyond either exposure alone and a measurable sequence linking delivery failure to metal availability and function. It does not predict that all medicines, all metals or all low-GSH measurements carry the same risk. [1] [11] [4] [13]

Proposed research · not a completed result

Separate impaired reserve from altered drug exposure

Use metabolically competent primary human hepatocytes from multiple donors in a factorial experiment: drug alone, chemically specified metal alone, both, and matched controls. Begin with acetaminophen and an iron-loading condition; investigate Cu(I)-handling and Cd(II) in separate arms. Predefine low-injury exposures from pilot pharmacokinetics, include a separate toxic-exposure positive control, and measure free-medium concentrations rather than equating culture concentrations with human dose. [4] [21] [1]

  • Resolve cytosolic and mitochondrial GSH/GSSG, isotope-labelled synthesis/import, SLC25A39 abundance, respiration and viability over time; quantify fraction purity and avoid oxidation during collection.
  • Measure parent-drug disappearance, major metabolites and protein adducts alongside total metal and orthogonal compartment-sensitive measures of exchangeable metal.
  • Compare partial SLC25A39 reduction with genetic restoration and a transport-deficient control; use a separate intervention on metal trafficking, verifying that it does not simply suppress drug bioactivation.
  • Predefine an interaction model and donor replication. Only then add a controlled microbial metabolite or infection condition to test whether the host mechanism remains predictive.

What would change the interpretation. The reserve hypothesis weakens if combined injury is merely additive, mitochondrial GSH/import remains unchanged, or restoring import fails despite confirmed correction of the pool. If altered drug exposure explains the effect, revise the mechanism to pharmacokinetics. If metal redistribution follows rather than precedes damage and blocking it does not help, it is a consequence rather than the proposed amplifier.

A field note worth sharing

Normal total glutathione can conceal a mitochondrial shortfall, as SLC25A39 knockout experiments showed.

Drug–metal research should measure delivery and chemical species, not assume a whole-cell antioxidant measurement reveals every vulnerable pool. [1]

Demonstrated in experimental mammalian cell systems; this does not establish mitochondrial glutathione failure during ordinary medicine use.

Questions worth asking

Is glutathione a metal chelator or an antioxidant?

It can participate in both metal coordination and redox chemistry, while also serving as an enzyme substrate and forming organic conjugates. The reaction, metal species, location and subsequent transport determine the effect; binding alone does not prove elimination.

Are all medicines that use glutathione risky because they deplete it?

No. Using a metabolic substrate does not establish depletion or injury. The important variables include the fraction using that route, reactive-product formation, replenishment, compartmental delivery and measured outcomes. Busulfan parent metabolism and acetaminophen NAPQI detoxification illustrate different cases.

Does a normal whole-cell glutathione result exclude mitochondrial deficiency?

No. Experimental SLC25A39 loss separates mitochondrial from whole-cell GSH. This compartment distinction does not validate a routine clinical test for mitochondrial reserve.

Does acetaminophen injury prove that drugs release heavy metals?

The evidence concerns endogenous iron in a toxic-exposure mouse-cell model. It does not establish ordinary-use human effects or a universal metal-release mechanism.
