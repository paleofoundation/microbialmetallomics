Americium & microbes

Microbial accumulation and biosorption

Why it belongs in microbial metallomics

Americium research in microbes is mainly about binding and partitioning rather than a demonstrated biological redox cycle. Bacteria, fungal biomass, and dissolved microbial products can change how americium distributes between water, cells, and mineral surfaces. [1][2]

What microbes do with it

  • Laboratory work with Escherichia coli and marine bacteria found that americium association depended on the organism and culture conditions. Growth medium and pH are therefore part of the result, not interchangeable technical details. [1]
  • Rhizopus arrihizus biomass rapidly adsorbed americium from test solutions. This is evidence for a sorbent: accumulation alone does not demonstrate a nutritional role or energy-yielding metabolism. [2]
  • Immobilizing the fungal biomass improved its handling characteristics and enabled repeated adsorption–desorption experiments. Engineering the physical form of biomass can be as relevant as identifying a binding organism. [3]

Experiments worth knowing

Immobilized Rhizopus arrihizus; laboratory solutions

Making fungal biomass reusable

Study design. Fungal biomass was embedded in a matrix and assessed through repeated adsorption–desorption cycles.

What they found. The immobilized material retained strong americium adsorption while offering improved mechanical and handling properties relative to free biomass. [3]

How to read it. Recoverability and reuse are distinct performance questions from whether a microorganism binds a metal at all.

Community & host evidence

Microbes can also influence the dissolved phase. In the bacterial study, cell-free culture filtrates reduced americium transfer to clay, consistent with microbial products altering partitioning. Thus, a microbe may supply both binding surfaces and compounds that keep metal away from other surfaces. [1]

Exposure & chemical form

The fungal experiments tested defined aqueous systems, including acidic conditions. Their adsorption performance cannot be assumed for seawater, mixed waste, or a living host. Immobilization changes how a sorbent can be recovered, but the captured americium remains in the collected material. [2][3]

What the evidence does not establish

These studies establish useful laboratory mechanisms, not biological destruction of americium or a proven field cleanup method. A full assessment needs to follow the loaded sorbent, desorption liquids, competing ions, and the stability of retention. High initial capture is only one part of that accounting. [2][3]

Open research questions

Questions suggested by the evidence and its limits.

  • Which dissolved microbial products increase americium mobility in the presence of clay or other minerals? [1]
  • How do competing ions affect capture and release over repeated sorbent-use cycles? [3]
