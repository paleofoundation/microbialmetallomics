Why it belongs in microbial metallomics

Molybdenum enables microbial reactions through specialized cofactors, including respiratory molybdoenzymes and the distinct FeMo cofactor of nitrogenase. These pathways connect metal availability with anaerobic growth and environmental nitrogen cycling. [1][2][3]

What microbes do with it

  • During experimental intestinal inflammation, host-derived nitrate gave E. coli a respiratory growth advantage over obligate anaerobes in mice. [1]
  • Molybdenum-cofactor-dependent respiratory pathways supported inflammation-associated Enterobacteriaceae expansion; tungstate interfered with these pathways in culture and mouse experiments. [2]
  • The common nitrogenase contains an FeMo cofactor. Comparison with vanadium nitrogenase demonstrates that replacing the heterometal changes cofactor structure and catalytic properties. [3]

Community & host evidence

The gut evidence highlighted here comes from mouse inflammation models and bacterial genetic experiments. It establishes a role for respiratory metabolism, not a demonstration that dietary molybdenum causes inflammatory bowel disease in people. [1][2]

Exposure & chemical form

Molybdenum-containing cofactors must be assembled within the organism; an environmental or dietary total-metal measurement does not establish the amount entering active respiratory enzymes. [2][3]

What the evidence does not establish

Respiratory advantage depends on electron acceptors and local conditions. Gene presence, total molybdenum, and molybdoenzyme activity are separate measurements. [1][2]
